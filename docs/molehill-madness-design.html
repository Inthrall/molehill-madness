Part I

The pitch

01Overview

Molehill Madness is a turn-based artillery game in the Worms tradition, with one structural change that touches everything: everybody takes their turn at the same time.

It is a four-player free-for-all by default. Each player commands a platoon of four moles, so sixteen moles share the field, and only one platoon walks away. Every round, each player picks one of their moles and plots its next eight seconds by walking a ghost of that mole through the plan while the mole itself stands still and watches. All four plans lock in together and play out simultaneously on one shared clock. Shots have to lead moving targets, four ambushes collide mid-air, and the best-laid plan marches proudly into a crater that did not exist when it was drawn.

Simultaneous turns are not a gimmick here; they are what makes four players possible at all. In a sequential artillery game, sixteen moles means sixteen turns per cycle and three people watching. Here, a four-player round takes exactly as long as a two-player round: 45 seconds of everyone planning at once, then 8 seconds of everyone watching at once. Nobody waits, ever.

Moving is one gesture. There is no dig button and no burrow mode: you drag a route wherever you want it and the mole takes it, walking where there is air and clawing where there is dirt, at the same pace throughout. Dirt is not slower, it is dearer, and the stamina bar is the whole explanation.

Three commitments shape everything else. It is built to be all ages: nobody dies, moles go comically off duty and turn up in the field hospital playing cards, and there is no free text chat anywhere. It is built to be wordless: nothing inside the game is written down, so it is playable before you can read, in any language, with no tutorial text to translate. And it is built to be shared: because a round is a deterministic replay of a few kilobytes, one button turns it into a vertical video with no UI in shot, ready for the share sheet in a few seconds.

It is also multiplayer only. There is no campaign, no puzzle mode and no AI to practise against, because everything worth playing for here comes from a person on the other side making a plan you have to guess at. You get into a game three ways: sitting next to somebody, typing in the game code a friend read out, or pressing one button for a random match if you are old enough for strangers (§12).

On PC it is couch-first: while everyone plots, the screen splits so each player has their own camera, then merges into one shared view for the fireworks. It is pocket-sized on mobile. It plays online at two paces on the same protocol: Live, with a shared planning timer, and Anytime, played like correspondence chess: commit whenever suits you, inside a day at most. And at round eight of every match, a line of lava appears along the bottom of the map and starts climbing, then closes in from both sides, until there is nowhere left to be careful.

03Design pillars

Every feature request gets tested against these five. If it fails two of them, it is out.

Everyone plays at once

Planning is parallel, resolution is shared, and the round is the same 53 seconds whether two are playing or four. Downtime, the artillery genre's oldest problem, is designed out rather than tolerated.

The dirt is the game

Terrain is cover, weapon, and road. Every crater rewrites the tunnel map, and everything you break stays broken.

Plans meet reality

You commit to intentions, not outcomes. Robust plans beat greedy ones, and with four plans colliding, the misfires are the comedy engine.

Losing is the best bit

A knocked-out mole gets a slapstick exit and a bed in the field hospital. The funniest thing that happens to you should be something going wrong, and it should be one button to send it to a friend.

Readable without reading

Every weapon telegraphs, every cost is a gauge, and nothing in the game is written in words (damage digits are the one numeral). Depth comes from prediction rather than execution, so pads compete fairly with mouse and keyboard, and a child who cannot read yet competes fairly with an adult.

Part II

Systems

04The moles

Every player fields a platoon of four moles, at every player count. A full four-player match therefore starts with sixteen moles on the field, which is the siege the game is designed around.

  • Pluck: 100 points per mole, the game's word for health, shown as a filling mole-head glyph rather than a number. Typical weapon damage lands between 15 and 45, so a mole lasts two to four good hits and a match is a story, not an execution.
  • Stamina: 100 points per mole per round, refilled every round. It is the movement budget and the only thing separating walking from tunnelling (§9).
  • Rotation: at the start of each round you pick any of your moles that has not acted in the current cycle; once all your available moles have acted, your cycle resets. This is the Worms auto-cycle made explicit, and which mole you pick is part of the plan, so it stays hidden until resolution in online play.
  • Going off duty: a mole at zero pluck is out of the match, comically and harmlessly, and reappears in the field hospital on the results screen (§5). Last platoon standing wins. With four moles each, being knocked out of a four-player match early is rare, which is the main reason platoon size does not scale down.
  • Verbs: move, hop, aim, fire, brace. Five, every one legible at a glance and at quarter-screen. Digging is deliberately not a verb: it is just what moving through dirt looks like.

No classes, ever

There are no Sappers, Gunners or Scouts, and there never will be. Every mole in the game has the same body, the same stamina, and access to the same arsenal, which means any mole can be the hero of any match. The one who survives on 4 pluck and lands the last shot was not built for it in a menu; the match made them.

Two systems exist to make that heroism stick:

  • Mole of the Match. The end-of-match screen picks out one mole from the whole field, by whatever it actually did (most damage, longest tunnel, luckiest survival, worst self-inflicted disaster) and awards it a generated title: Private Nibbles, Hero of the Third Flowerbed.
  • Names persist. Platoon rosters are yours across matches, so the mole who won you a match last week is the one you send in first today, entirely for sentimental reasons and with no mechanical benefit whatsoever.
Fantasy check

The fantasy is tiny military earnestness: helmets a size too big, trench periscopes, tea breaks in dugouts, four platoons who each believe the flowerbed is rightfully theirs. The moles take the war extremely seriously. The game never does.

05Going off duty

No mole ever dies. When a mole runs out of pluck it is knocked out of the match by a piece of slapstick, and the animation is the reward for the hit rather than a punishment for the loss. This is a headline feature, not a content compromise: it is the single biggest lever on the age rating (§18) and the single most shareable second of any round (§15).

Eight hand-animated exits ship at launch, picked deterministically from the sim state so every client and every replay shows the same one. The choice keys off how the mole went out, so the animation reads as a consequence rather than a shuffle.

Rules the reel follows

  • Nothing is ever damaged, only inconvenienced. No blood, no bandages, no soot-blackened faces, no X-ed-out eyes, no ragdolls. Moles leave under their own dignity or someone else's stretcher.
  • Every exit is legible at quarter-screen. Each one has a distinct silhouette read (spin, carry, tip, inflate, punch-through, launch, drop, pop) so a player watching a 960×540 quadrant knows what happened without reading a number.
  • Roughly 1.2 seconds, and the sim does not wait. Knockouts play out inside the 8-second resolution alongside everything else. A four-way knockout is four simultaneous pratfalls, which is exactly the kind of thing the clip button exists for.
  • Nothing is left behind. No bodies, no markers, no rubble where a mole used to be. The field after a knockout looks exactly as it did before, minus one mole and plus a great deal of dignity lost.
  • The field hospital. The results screen shows every knocked-out mole from all four platoons in a tent, bandage-free, eating grubs and playing cards, with the ones who went out first now comfortably winning at cards. It is the game's way of saying nothing bad happened.
Why this is load-bearing

An artillery game's core loop is watching things go wrong. If going wrong is grim, the game needs an older rating and the clips do not travel. If going wrong is a pratfall, the same mechanic produces an all-ages rating, a share-worthy 8 seconds, and a reason to keep playing after you lose. One animation reel pays for three separate design goals.

06The round

A match is a sequence of rounds, and every round has the same four beats. Every player is active in all four, and the round takes the same time regardless of how many are playing: adding players adds lanes, not minutes.

  1. Plan (45 s, shared timer): every player picks an eligible mole and plots its turn (§7). Anyone may commit early; the round resolves when everyone has committed or the timer ends.
  2. Commit: all plans lock simultaneously. A player who plans nothing braces in place, which is the safe default for a dropped connection or a distracted friend.
  3. Resolve (up to 8 s): every plan replays together in the shared world (§8).
  4. Aftermath (4 s): damage tallies per player, knockouts, the clip button for the round just played, next round's crates telegraphed, and in Boiling Point the lava creeps up. Then back to planning.

07Ghost planning

Planning is done by walking a ghost of your mole through the turn and handing that recording to the game. The critical presentation rule: the mole itself never moves during planning. It stays exactly where it is, fully solid, and a ghost-filtered copy of that same mole does the walking.

How the ghost looks and behaves

  • It is the same mole, not a marker. The ghost is that mole's own sprite, outfit and helmet, rendered at about 45% opacity with a cool rim light and a faint shimmer. It reads as "this mole, eight seconds from now", never as a different character or an abstract cursor.
  • The real mole stays solid and in place, idling, and turns its head to watch its own ghost walk away. That small piece of characterisation is also the functional point: your starting position stays visible, so you can judge how far you have actually committed to travelling.
  • After-images drop at each waypoint and fade, so a glance shows the shape of the route without reading the line.
  • Walked and still-to-come are drawn differently. Behind the looping ghost the route is a solid line, ahead of it dashed; the split travels with the ghost as it re-walks the plan.
  • Nothing about the ghost touches the simulation. It is a preview rendered from the same sim run forward speculatively; the world state is untouched until commit.

The plan is laid in ink

There is no timeline editing, no scrubbing back, no nudging a waypoint. You lay the plan forward only, like drawing with a pen, and everything happens at the pen's tip.

  • Drag from your mole and the route flows out of the tip, snapping along walkable surface and straight through diggable dirt. While your finger (or stick, or mouse) is still down you can back the pen up along its own stroke; the moment you lift, that ink is dry.
  • Actions happen at the tip. A tap adds a hop or a brace where the pen currently is. Hold fire to aim (the threat preview draws the arc and blast radius against the world as it exists now), release to stamp the shot there and then. The dynamite button plants its bundle at the tip (§10). Nothing can be inserted behind the tip, ever.
  • The timeline is a gauge, not an editor. A bar along the bottom fills as the route spends its 8 seconds; the stamina bar drains beside it (§9). When either runs out, the pen simply stops. Meanwhile the ghost walks the laid plan on a loop, so what you have actually committed to is always in front of you.
  • One shot per turn, and it can only be stamped where the route is in open air, so the rule that you cannot fire from inside dirt is enforced by the interface rather than explained by it. Once stamped, it is stamped.
  • One reset per turn. Hold the reset control and the whole plan wipes, the ghost snapping back to the standing mole. The button carries a single token that greys out when spent, and supply crates drop spares (§11). That is the entire undo system: fluff a route twice and you are taking the second version into battle. The mistakes add to the chaos, and the chaos is the game.

What makes it a mind game

  • You are aiming at the future. Your target will be mid-plan when your shell lands, so you fire at where they will be, not where they are. Leading, area denial, and cutting off escape tunnels are the skill ceiling.
  • Three futures, not one. In a four-player match you are predicting three opponents at once, which changes the maths: the safest plan is rarely the one that dodges a specific threat, it is the one that is hard for anybody to punish. Positional play beats reads as the table fills up.
  • The map is the only record. No route history is drawn over the world and no opponent's past turn is replayed for you. What you get instead is the terrain itself: every tunnel anyone has dug is still there, so the map is a readable account of where people have been and what they were trying to reach. Reading dirt is a skill; reading an overlay is not.
  • On the couch, plans are public. Every pane is visible to everyone, and other players' ghosts appear in your view too, tinted their platoon colour and fainter than your own. Peeking is not cheating, it is the game, but acting on what you see costs your only reset, so the question all planning phase is whether what you just spotted is worth the redo. Committing early is itself a bluff, because your ghost freezes where it stands. Online, ghosts are private until resolution.
Design rule

The plan is a recording of inputs (steer toward the next waypoint, fire at t=5.2 s), never a teleport list of positions. This single rule is what makes simultaneous resolution honest, and it is why plans degrade comically instead of breaking when the world changes underneath them.

08Simultaneous resolution

Resolution is one deterministic replay of every input recording on a single clock. The rules below are the contract with the player, and they are worth printing on the wall:

  • No initiative, ever. There is no "player 1 resolves first", at any player count. All physics, damage and knockouts in a simulation tick apply simultaneously. Four-way mutual knockouts stand, and they are glorious.
  • Plans survive surprises, not pain. If a crater appears under your planned path, your mole keeps steering toward it: falls in, climbs out, carries on with whatever dignity remains. But the moment a mole takes damage of any kind, its recording is torn up on the spot, exactly like original Worms: movement stops, the unfired shot is cancelled, and physics (knockback, tumbling, lava bounces) decides the rest of its eight seconds.
  • Shots fire as scheduled, if you make it there intact. Your weapon fires at its timestamp, at its committed angle and power, from wherever you actually are, so being punted off your firing ledge at t=4.9 s sends your t=5.2 s mortar somewhere very interesting. And because damage cancels recordings, hitting somebody before their firing timestamp deletes their shot entirely. Firing early is insurance; firing late is greed. This is the deepest read in the game, and it emerges from two rules rather than a system.
  • Collisions are soft and order-free. Moles are round; any number arriving at the same spot bump and shove through the same symmetric solver, so the outcome cannot depend on player number. No clipping, no stacking, no priority.
  • Contested pickups: first collision by sim tick wins a crate. A two-way tie splits the contents. A three- or four-way tie shatters the crate in the scramble and nobody gets anything, which is both deterministic and correct.
  • Friendly fire is always on, and there is no toggle. Every mole takes full damage from everything, including the rest of your own platoon standing admiringly close to your shot. It is funnier that way, and one rule with no exceptions is also one rule to teach.
  • Shared randomness. Anything random (crate contents, bounce jitter, which knockout animation plays) draws from one seeded stream every client agrees on, fixed at commit. Replays are bit-exact, pratfalls included.

Balance and the free-for-all problem

Balance follows one axis: the harder an attack is to dodge, the less it pays. Ballistics can be side-stepped by a good read, so they hit hard. Seismic weapons punish through the ground and cannot really be dodged, so they chip. The melee mallet demands you predict a mole's exact position at an exact moment, so it pays out the most in damage and comedy.

Four-player free-for-all brings two known problems, and the design answers both rather than pretending they are features:

  • Ganging up on the leader. There are no in-game alliances, but players make them at the sofa, and the person in front eats three shells. This is dampened, not removed: no mid-match score table is shown beyond what is visible on the field, so "who is winning" stays arguable; the telegraphed crates (§11) give everyone a shared objective to fight over instead; and Boiling Point compresses the endgame so a pile-on has less time to decide the match. It stays on the risk register (§20) as the thing playtesting must watch hardest.
  • Four shots in eight seconds is a lot to read. Presentation carries this, not the rules: the camera merges when the action is close enough to share a frame and stays split into per-player panes when it is not (§13), the final significant impact plays in brief slow motion, damage numbers pop in-world as hits land, and instant replay re-runs the round from any angle. The simulation is untouched by all of it.

09Movement, dirt and lava

Terrain is a per-cell destructible bitmap, exactly as fine-grained as classic Worms, rendered as chunky hand-drawn soil. Moles add the twist: the map is traversable on both sides of the surface, and moving between them is not an action.

One move, one speed, one cost that changes

There is no dig button, no burrow mode and no surfacing animation to sit through. You drag a route and the mole follows it; where the route lies in open air the mole walks, and where it passes through dirt the mole's claws come out and it keeps going at the same pace. Speed never changes. Only the price does.

That single rule is what makes the stamina bar the most important gauge on the screen, and it means the whole surface-versus-underground trade can be taught without a word of tutorial text: you drag a route into the dirt, you watch the bar fall off a cliff, you understand.

What the dirt does to the fight

  • Tunnels persist for the whole match. By round ten the cross-section reads like an ant farm, and because nothing else records where players have been, that dug history is the only intelligence anyone gets (§7).
  • Underground trade-off: a buried mole is immune to ballistics but exposed to Fracking, collapses and the rising lava, and it cannot fire, because a shot needs open air (§7). The Tunnel Torpedo is the one thing that reaches out of the dirt.
  • Height is a weapon nobody has to carry. A mole that comes off the roof of a cave, or is thrown off a ledge by somebody else's blast, is charged for the landing. Knockback is therefore not a free ride, the sixty metres between the top of the map and the bottom of it mean something, and blowing the floor out from under an opponent is a shot in its own right rather than a way of rearranging where they stand.
  • Craters deny movement. Because travel happens through dirt, blowing dirt up removes roads as well as cover, and blowing a tunnel open turns a cheap highway into an expensive detour. Area weapons are route weapons, which keeps every shot interesting even when it misses.
  • Stamina is per round and refills. There is no fatigue to manage across turns; the only lasting consequences of a route are the tunnel it leaves behind and whoever saw you make it.

Boiling Point: a rising line, then a closing box

For most of the match there is no lava at all. There are no molten pockets to dig into, nothing to divert, nothing to set into rock. Lava is not a terrain material; it is the match clock, and it behaves in exactly one way.

  • It arrives all at once, at round 8, every match. A fixed round rather than a formula: eight rounds is enough time to dig one good idea and steal one crate, and after that the map itself starts asking for a winner.
  • It climbs one step every round, during the aftermath beat, so everybody sees the next step coming before they plan against it.
  • It fills the tunnels first, because it comes from below. The underground, the safe option all match, becomes the first thing to go, and everybody is pushed up onto the exposed surface together.
  • Past the halfway mark it closes in from the sides. Once the line reaches the middle of the map, lava also begins creeping in from the left and right edges each round, so the playable area becomes a box that shrinks from three directions at once. There is nowhere to run out the clock.
  • It is bouncy, and you get two warnings. A mole that lands on lava is trampolined back out on a burst of steam, yelping, for 10 pluck; real physics decides where it comes down, and the damage ends its recording (§8). A second landing bounces it again for another 10. The third landing is the Steam Pop and the mole is off duty (§5). Being knocked toward the lava is therefore survivable, occasionally twice, and a mole pinballing between two pools while its owner watches through their fingers is the game at its funniest.
  • Nothing is destroyed and nothing is left behind: terrain the lava reaches is simply gone from play.

Biomes for v1: Garden (lawns, flowerbeds, a sprinkler hazard), Allotment (root vegetables as chunky soft cover), and Building Site (pipes, concrete slabs, a cement mixer that drips new terrain). Each biome is a palette and a hazard, not a ruleset. None of them explains where the lava comes from, and none of them needs to.

Ammo follows Worms convention: staples are infinite, the spicy stuff is limited, and the last two are crate-only rarities that turn a match around. One of them, the dynamite, lives on its own button rather than the wheel (§13). Every one of them is silly on purpose, which is a rating decision as much as a tone decision (§18). 11Pressure and pacing

Simultaneous turns have a known failure mode: four cowards in four bunkers, each waiting for the others to blink. Three systems keep the fight moving toward contact, and all three scale with the number of players.

  • Telegraphed crates, landed in no-man's-land. Every aftermath announces exactly where next round's crates will land, and they land mid-resolution at t=4 s. The spawn is never neutral ground by accident: crates land centrally, as close to equidistant from every platoon as the terrain allows, on the surface or punching a small hole where they hit, embedding a metre down so somebody has to dig the last stretch. Two players get one crate; three or four get two, so the fight has more than one flashpoint. Crates carry the limited weapons, 25-pluck grub snacks, Boom Beets restocks and spare reset tokens (§7), so a telegraphed crate is a fight everybody scheduled in advance.
  • Pillowforts are legal. The game never scolds a turtler, never pings a "get moving" warning, never docks points for cowardice. It just keeps landing everything worth having in the middle of the map, and eventually sends the lava. Engagement is encouraged by geography, not by nagging.
  • Boiling Point, at round 8, every match. The lava arrives and begins to climb, then to close in from the sides (§9). A fixed round means every match has the same three acts, everyone at the table can count toward it, and the shrinking box guarantees a match cannot be waited out by anybody.
  • Stalemate nudge. Three consecutive rounds with zero damage dealt anywhere on the field shrinks every platoon's stamina budget by 10%: bunkers slowly become too expensive to maintain. Gentle, thematic, and it only bites a table that is collectively refusing to play.

All three pressures lean the same way: toward contact, toward the surface, and toward a finish. A player who spends a whole match tunnelling defensively should find, somewhere around round twenty, that the game has quietly taken the option away.

Part III

Platforms and technology

12Game modes

The game is multiplayer only. There is no campaign, no puzzle mode, no trick-shot challenge run and no practice match against a machine. Four modes ship, and every one of them is people against people: two ways to play in a room, two ways to play apart.

This is a deliberate cut rather than a gap left for later. Everything interesting in the design comes from a person on the other side making a plan you have to guess at, and a solo mode is either that experience faked badly or a different game wearing the same art. Building neither buys the whole team's attention for the part that matters. It does, however, put two things on the critical path that a solo mode would normally cover: teaching the game (§13) and having somebody to play at 11pm on a Tuesday, which is what Anytime pace and matchmaking exist to answer.

  • The couch needs neither. Local play wants no account, no code and no connection. Plug in the pads and start.

13Screens and controls

The screen has exactly two states, and the transition between them is a signature moment worth animating with care. The planning state divides by player count, with one shared timer sitting in the middle of the screen, straddling the seam, so the countdown belongs to the table rather than to any pane. The resolution state depends on the fight: one merged camera when the acting moles are close enough to share a readable frame, per-player panes when they are far apart, each pane replaying its owner's part of the round. The merge is earned by proximity, not forced by the format.

A UI without words

No words inside the game, anywhere. No labels, no tooltips, no weapon names, no ability text. Every quantity is a gauge, every object is drawn as itself, and every rule is enforced by what the interface will and will not let you do. The one numeral on screen is the damage digit, which is language-free and earns its place below.

  • Weapons are objects, not names. The selection wheel shows the acorn, the mallet, the mousetrap, the derrick, the gnome, each drawn large and distinct. You learn what a thing is by firing it once, which takes about a second and is more fun than reading about it.
  • Damage numbers are the one numeral on by default. Digits are language-neutral, so they cost the wordless goal nothing, and reading exactly what a hit was worth is half the pleasure of an artillery game. They pop in-world above the struck mole, in its platoon's colour, in matches and clips alike; the mole-head glyphs empty to match. Written words stay banned everywhere.
  • Rules are enforced, not stated. You cannot stamp a shot inside dirt, so nobody has to be told you cannot fire from underground. You cannot drag a route into bedrock. The pen simply stops when time or stamina runs out. Every rule in this document should be discoverable by trying it.
  • Communication is pictures. The emote wheel is the only channel, and it was already wordless for safety reasons (§18). Two decisions, one system.
  • Words survive only outside the match: the store, where prices are legally required, plus settings, the age gate, credits and legal text. The game itself has none.

What it buys and what it costs

  • It is playable before you can read, which is the difference between a game a family can play together and a game a parent has to narrate.
  • Localisation nearly disappears. Only the store, settings and legal screens need translating, so the game ships worldwide at once instead of trickling out in language batches. For a free game that lives on shared clips, that matters commercially as much as it does ethically.
  • Clips need no subtitles and read identically in every country, which is exactly what a short-video platform rewards.
  • Teaching is the real cost, and there is nowhere to put it. Nothing can be explained in a sentence, and with no solo modes (§12) there is no puzzle ladder to hide a tutorial inside. The first round of a real match has to do the whole job. See below.
  • Icon design is a first-class deliverable, not a late pass. Every glyph must survive quarter-screen on a TV, a phone at arm's length, and a portrait clip (§17).
  • Accessibility keeps the words available. A "show labels" setting turns on names, numerals and tooltips for players who want explicit information, and every control carries a screen-reader label regardless. It is off by default and must never quietly become the real interface the wordless one is decorating.

Teaching it with no tutorial to put it in

A wordless game with no solo modes has nowhere to hide a tutorial, so the first round of a real match has to do the whole job. It can, and the reason is the turn model: everybody plans at the same time, so a beginner fumbling through their first plan holds nobody up. A new player gets 45 unhurried seconds in which nothing they do is real yet, three other people busy with their own screens, and no way to embarrass themselves. That is a better teaching environment than most tutorials manage.

  • A fumbled first plan is content, not failure. The ink model means beginners will lay bad routes, and the design absorbs that: one reset forgives the worst of it, and whatever survives resolves into slapstick rather than punishment. Nothing needs to be labelled "practice", because a bad turn is already the game working.
  • There is one gesture. On your first planning screen a drawn paw performs the drag once, slowly, and then gets out of the way. Everything else in the game is a button at the pen's tip.
  • The ghost answers "what happens if". Every consequence is previewed before you commit it, which is exactly what a tutorial's words would otherwise have to describe (§7).
  • The gauges teach the economy in one stroke. Push the pen into dirt, watch the stamina bar fall off a cliff, back the pen out before you lift. That is the entire surface-versus-underground lesson and it takes four seconds (§9).
  • The interface refuses rather than explains. Shots will not drop inside dirt, routes will not enter bedrock, unaffordable tails grey out as you drag. Nobody has to be told a rule they cannot break.
  • First matches are seeded together where the population allows, so a beginner's first game is against other beginners rather than somebody who has been reading dirt for six months.
  • The couch is still the best teacher. Most people will learn this game from the person sitting next to them, which is one more reason local play is the flagship rather than a bonus.
Highest-risk UX in the project

Removing the solo modes removed the tutorial with them, and this is where that lands. The first round has to teach a wordless game to a stranger, in a live match, or they close it. It gets a dedicated spike at M2 and is tested on people who have never seen the game rather than on us. If it fails, the honest answer is a first-run experience, which is a solo mode wearing a different hat, and it should be built rather than argued about.

Couch specifics

  • Quadrants, not shared cameras. Plotting an eight-second path with waypoints needs personal zoom, so each player keeps their own view. On a 1080p TV a quadrant is 960×540, which is larger than a Switch handheld screen, and the art is drawn with bold outlines precisely so it survives the shrink (§17).
  • Three players use the same quadrant grid, with the fourth cell given over to a wide view of the whole map: the crate telegraph, everyone's position, and how far the lava has climbed. No gauge is needed for the lava, because the lava is visible.
  • The shared timer lives at the centre of the screen in every planning layout, straddling the seam between panes (or the quadrant cross), so the countdown reads as the table's clock rather than any one player's.
  • Devices: any mix of up to four controllers, plus one mouse-and-keyboard seat. Joining is by pressing a button on an idle device at the lobby. There are no AI opponents anywhere in the game, so a player who walks away mid-match simply retires their platoon and the rest fight on.
  • Steam Deck Verified is a launch target for online play; the Deck's screen takes two panes comfortably and four only in a pinch, so four-player couch play is flagged as TV-first in the store copy.

Input

  • Mouse and keyboard: drag to lay the ink, wheel to zoom, right-drag to pan; left click stamps actions at the tip and the same fire/dynamite/reset keys mirror the buttons. The precision option.
  • Controller: cursor with magnetic snap to surfaces; the stick lays the ink. The pad mirrors the phone's controls: right stick flicks the wheel, right trigger holds to aim and releases to stamp, right bumper plants the dynamite, B holds for the one reset. Tuned first, because most couch seats will be pads.
  • Touch (the layout in the figure above): landscape only, and the whole scheme is one drag and four controls.
    • Drag on the field to lay the route in ink; pinch to zoom. While the finger is down the pen can back up along its own stroke; lift it and the ink dries. The gauge along the bottom fills as the route spends its 8 seconds, and the ghost re-walks the plan on a loop by itself; there is nothing to scrub.
    • The weapon wheel sits on the right edge: flick up or down to cycle through the objects, current one drawn largest. No menu ever opens over the field.
    • Fire sits under the right thumb: press and hold to aim (drag bends the arc, the preview updates live), release to stamp the shot at the pen's tip. Once stamped, it is stamped; the only way to take a shot back is the reset.
    • Dynamite sits beside fire: one press plants the Boom Beets at the tip, fuse already fizzing in the plan. It does not spend the turn's shot, and the button shows how many bundles the platoon has left (§10).
    • Reset sits under the left thumb: hold for half a second (a ring drains around it, the wordless confirm) and the whole plan wipes, the ghost snapping back to the standing mole. It works once per turn: the token on the button greys out when spent, and crates drop spares (§11). A tap does nothing, so no plan dies to a stray touch.
    • Stamina and the round timer ride the top edge, with pluck and rival glyphs beside them, because thumbs own the bottom corners and must never cover a gauge.
    Every tap target is at least 44 pt; the plan, not the player's fingers, needs the precision, which is why prediction-based depth (§3) matters so much on mobile.

14Online play: one protocol, two paces, up to four seats

Simultaneous turns collapse online multiplayer into something small: a round is just the exchange of a few tiny plan payloads. There is no realtime state sync, no rollback, no server-side physics. Every client replays the same deterministic sim (§16) and is guaranteed to agree. Adding players adds payloads, not complexity, which is why four-player online costs lobby UI rather than architecture.

Why four-player async actually works

Correspondence play normally degrades badly with player count, because a sequential game needs one turn per player before the board moves. Simultaneous planning removes that entirely: the 24-hour window belongs to the round, not to a player, so everyone plans inside the same day and a four-way match advances at exactly the same rate as a duel.

Hosting and game codes

  • Whoever opens the lobby is the host. The host picks the player count (2, 3 or 4) and the pace up front, and the game answers with a short code, five letters, Kahoot-style. The lobby fills to the chosen number and starts. No votes, no negotiating a maximum after the fact: "come play a duel" and "come make up a four" are simply different lobbies.
  • One code joins every platform. Typing five letters works identically on a PC, a phone, a tablet, or any console this ever reaches, which is exactly why it is a code rather than a URL: no share sheets, no platform friend systems, no accounts to look up. A tappable link that pre-fills the code exists for chat apps, and it is the same system clip links ride (§15), so the growth loop and the invitation loop stay one piece of infrastructure.
  • Empty seats stay empty. A lobby that never fills waits, or the host lowers the count and starts. There are no AI seat-fillers, because a game whose entire depth is reading a human being is not improved by handing a seat to something that is not one.
  • Random matchmaking ships at launch, not as a later addition, because with no solo modes it is the only way to play when nobody you know is around. One pool for Steam and mobile, one button, no ranking to start with. Age-gated, per §18.
  • Cross-play accounts: lightweight account (email or platform sign-in). Fairness across devices holds because nothing rewards twitch (§3).

Timers, dropouts and cheating

  • Live pace: everyone connected over a socket, shared 60 s planning timer, resolution plays the moment the last commit lands. A player who disconnects has their mole brace; if they do not return within a round the match downgrades to Anytime pace rather than dying, which is a genuinely novel courtesy this architecture gets for free.
  • Anytime pace: commit whenever suits you inside the round's window, which is 24 hours at the most (the host can set it shorter when opening the lobby). Miss the window and your platoon forfeits on the spot; the match carries on between whoever is left. No grace tokens, no zombie matches limping on for a fortnight: a day of silence is an answer. Several matches run in parallel, and push notifications fire on resolution rather than on each opponent's commit, so a four-way match is at most one notification a day.
  • Cheating: v1 relies on the payload being inputs, so you cannot submit an illegal state, only illegal inputs, which every client's sim rejects identically. Post-v1, a headless copy of the same sim library replays flagged match logs server-side; divergence is proof.
  • No text chat, anywhere. Communication is a fixed wheel of emotes and canned phrases, which is a deliberate safety decision rather than a missing feature (§18).

15Clips and sharing

Every round is already a deterministic replay of a few kilobytes, which means the game can re-render it as a clean video instead of capturing the screen. That one fact is what makes a genuine single-press share possible on a phone: no recording, no cropping, no UI in shot, no editing app.

The target: from the aftermath screen, one press produces a vertical video in the share sheet in under five seconds.

What one press does

  • Picks the moment for you. The sim scores the round for drama (damage dealt, mutual knockouts, Steam Pops, near misses, spectacular own goals) and clips the highest-scoring thread. One press means the game makes the editorial decision, and it names the clip too: Mutual knockout, Own goal of the day, Bronzed by his own tunnel.
  • Frames it for a phone. A side-on battlefield is a terrible fit for 9:16, so portrait clips are not a crop of the game view. A dedicated portrait camera opens on a half-second wide establishing shot, then pushes in and tracks the chosen thread's action centroid. Square 1:1 and landscape 16:9 are the same pipeline with different framing rules.
  • Keeps it short. Half-second title card, the 8-second round, two-second outro with the result and a share link: about eleven seconds, comfortably inside every platform's short-video sweet spot.
  • Leaves the UI out, keeps the world in. No timers, no gauges, no pane dividers. What stays is what lives in the world: mole and platoon names over their owners, and damage digits as hits land, because they are generated names and universal numbers, not personal data (§18). Account names never appear. Just moles, dirt, numbers and consequences, with a small corner watermark.
  • Offers the whole round too. A second tap switches from the auto-chosen thread to the full four-player round in landscape, for the group chat that wants the whole picture.

Why this is the growth loop

  • The caption carries a share link that opens the actual interactive replay in the game, or in a lightweight web player for people who do not have it yet. It is the same code-and-link system the match lobbies use (§12), so a clip is an advert, a demo, an install link and an invitation in one, and the funniest thing that happened to you is doing the marketing (§19).
  • Every clip is already localised. The game contains no words (§13), so a clip needs no subtitles, no regional cut and no translation pass. The same eleven seconds works in every market on the day it is made, which is exactly the property a short-video platform rewards and the one most games have to pay for.
  • Cosmetics are in every frame. Your platoon's outfits and weapon reskins are the most-seen art in any shared clip, which is the honest commercial reason the cosmetics-only model works here.
  • Knockouts are the payload. The knockout reel (§5) was designed for this: eight distinct, legible, funny 1.2-second beats, which is exactly the unit a short-video platform rewards.
  • On PC: the same button, plus MP4 and animated GIF export to disk and Steam's own clip integration. Landscape by default there.
The real cost

Everything above is cheap except the last step. Getting rendered frames out of Godot and into the platform hardware encoder (MediaCodec on Android) is the one genuinely uncertain piece of engineering in this document, and the five-second target on a mid-range phone needs proving rather than assuming. It gets its own spike at M2, and a graceful fallback: if encoding cannot hit the target, the button shares an animated image plus the replay link while the video pipeline catches up.

16Engine and determinism

The architecture has one load-bearing rule: the game is a deterministic C# library, and Godot is a lens pointed at it.

  • MoleSim (working name) is a plain netstandard2.1 class library: fixed-point Q48.16 maths, integer terrain bitmap, custom circle-versus-terrain physics, seeded xoshiro RNG. No floats anywhere in simulation state; floats vary across CPUs and would silently fork a Live match between an iPhone and a PC.
  • Player count is a parameter. The sim takes a list of plans, not two named plans, and the collision and pickup solvers are order-independent by construction (§8). Supporting four seats is a lobby feature, not a simulation rewrite.
  • Tick model: the sim steps at a fixed 30 Hz and the renderer interpolates to whatever the display wants. A full 8 s resolution is 240 ticks regardless of player count, replayable in microseconds, which is what makes the ghost preview, instant replays and clip generation all cheap.
  • Terrain: a ~2500×1200 cell bitmap at 5 cm per cell (a field about 125 m wide and 60 m deep), marching-squares meshed per 64-cell chunk; a carve dirties only the chunks it touches. Each cell also carries a material id, and the entire movement economy is a per-material stamina cost looked up on that id (§9), which means retuning the whole game's pacing is editing a table of six numbers.
  • Lava is a number, not a fluid. It is a height value that increments each aftermath, plus two inset values once it passes halfway. Every cell below the line is simply out of play, and a mole touching the line takes a bounce impulse, 10 damage and a per-mole strike count; the third strike is the knockout (§9). There is no simulation of flow, no cooling state and nothing to tune beyond the step size and the bounce, which is the entire reason it was designed this way.
  • Knockout animations are presentation, but their selection is simulation. Which of the eight exits plays is decided in the sim from the damage type and the seeded stream, so every client and every clip shows the same pratfall.
  • Godot's job is rendering, UI, audio, juice, split-screen viewports, the offscreen clip viewport and platform plumbing. Nothing in Godot may mutate sim state except by submitting inputs.
  • Determinism CI: a corpus of recorded matches, including four-player ones, replays on every commit on Windows, Linux and Android builds; final-state hashes must match exactly. Grows with every bug found, forever.
  • Relay service: deliberately boring. REST plus a socket hub, match logs in a small database, FCM push, one small VM's worth of load. It stores and forwards; it does not think.
Known risk, hedged by design

Godot 4's C# export to Android is the youngest part of this stack; four-viewport split-screen and the video encoder bridge are the next two. The week-one spike settled the first one: the simulation computes byte-identical results on Windows, Linux and Android, across two processor architectures, at 60 fps on a real phone. Godot still labels .NET mobile export experimental, which is worth watching as the client grows; M1 spikes four live viewports at 60 fps; M2 spikes the encoder (§15). Because MoleSim has zero engine dependencies and the Godot layer is deliberately thin, the worst realistic outcome is swapping the lens, not reshooting the film.

Presentation, audience and production

17Art and audio

  • Look: hand-drawn 2D cartoon with confident chunky outlines, flat colour with soft grain, heavy squash-and-stretch. Moles are round, near-sighted, and dressed in military surplus a size too big: helmets over the eyes, goggles, the occasional regimental scarf. The style is chosen for slapstick first: exaggerated anticipation and follow-through are what make the knockout reel (§5) funny rather than merely brief.
  • Readability rules, doing triple duty. Silhouettes first: a braced mole, a digging mole and an aiming mole must read at 10% zoom on a phone, at quarter-screen on a TV, and at 1080×1920 in a clip watched at arm's length. That constraint is why the outlines are heavy and why platoon identity is carried by trim colour plus headgear shape.
  • Ghost treatment: the planning ghost is the mole's own sprite at 45% opacity with a cool rim light and a slow shimmer, never a wireframe or a silhouette, because it has to read as the same character (§7). Other players' ghosts take their platoon hue at a lower opacity again, so your own plan always reads brightest in your own pane.
  • Four platoon colours chosen for colour-blind separation as well as contrast against soil, each paired with a distinct helmet silhouette so the four never rely on hue alone. Ghosts, roster glyphs, hit reactions and damage digits all inherit the owning platoon's colour, and the digits get a hand-drawn numeral face of their own so the one number in the game still looks like the game.
  • The icon system is a deliverable, not a pass. With no words anywhere (§13), the glyph set is load-bearing art: weapon objects, the claw, the mole head, the emote wheel, the four helmets. Each is designed at three sizes and reviewed at quarter-screen before it is drawn properly.
  • Strata are readable, because they are prices. Loose soil, packed soil and root mat each get a distinct texture and value, since a player has to be able to guess what a route will cost before dragging it. This is the one place where the art is doing arithmetic.
  • The cross-section is the brand. Dirt renders like an ant-farm diorama: strata, roots, worm cameos (they are neutral civilians, they file complaints, and two of them moonlight as stretcher bearers). Tunnels get a darker lining so the whole history of the match reads at a glance.
  • Lava is the one bright thing on screen, and it is deliberately kept cheerful rather than menacing: a warm orange glow that spills onto nearby dirt, lazy bubbles, comic heat shimmer. No dark reds, no skull imagery, nothing that would read as frightening to a small child (§18).
  • Audio: a brass band that has had one shandy too many: parps, kazoos, a tuba sting for mutual knockouts, a slide whistle for the Balloon Exit. The signature trick is the underground filter: when the camera follows a digging mole below the grass line, the whole mix muffles like ears popping. Boiling Point adds a low bubbling churn that rises in the mix, so the table hears the clock without being frightened by it. Mole voices are mumbled Kiwi-inflected gibberish, recorded cheap, loved forever.
  • Haptics (mobile): a soft tick while scrubbing the timeline, a coarser one as a route crosses into dirt (the stamina cost, felt rather than read), a thud per Fracking pulse, and a long rumble each time the lava climbs.

18All ages by design

The goal is the lowest age rating we can honestly obtain: ESRB E and PEGI 3. That is ambitious for an artillery game, and the honest expectation is ESRB E with PEGI 7 as a likely floor in Europe, because PEGI reads any depicted violence toward animal-like characters strictly. That floor is accepted in advance: if PEGI says 7, the store copy says 7, and no weapon gets cut chasing the 3. The programme below is designed to give the boards nothing to catch on, and every item is a design decision already reflected elsewhere in this document rather than a late content pass.

Content levers

  • Nobody dies. Moles go off duty via slapstick and turn up in the field hospital playing cards (§5). No blood, no injury, no bandages, no soot, no X-ed eyes, no ragdolls, no death vocabulary anywhere in the UI: "off duty", "knocked out", "pluck" rather than health.
  • The weapons are toys. Clods of earth, indignant beetles, acorns, a fairground mallet, an oversized mousetrap, an oil derrick, a hawk in a postal cap and a three-tonne garden gnome (§10). No firearms, no realistic ordnance, no military branding. The one word that could have caused trouble, "bazooka", is already gone, and the one that remains, the Moly Hand Grenade, is drawn as a golden relic rather than anything you could buy.
  • The hazard is friendly. Lava launches you out of the match on a puff of steam; it is drawn warm and bubbly rather than hellish, it never burns anything, and it leaves nothing behind (§17).
  • Nothing is written, so nothing reads as dark. A wordless game has no threatening copy, no scoreboards that shame anyone, and nothing a child can misread (§13).
  • No frightening beats. No jump scares, no darkness, no threatening music, no distress vocalisations. The loudest emotional note in the game is a tuba.
  • Language: none stronger than "blast" and "bother", in mumbled gibberish anyway.

Safety and compliance levers

For a game aimed at all ages, the rating is the easy half. The regulatory half is player communication and data, and it is designed conservatively on purpose:

  • No free text chat, anywhere, ever. Not in lobbies, not in matches, not in clip captions. Communication is a fixed wheel of emotes and pre-written phrases, which cannot be used to harass, groom or leak personal information. This removes the single largest moderation liability in online play, and it is why the game can be marketed to families without a moderation team.
  • No user-authored text at all, permanently. Platoon and mole names come from a large curated generator with a reroll button, full stop; there is no text field to add later and no moderation pipeline to staff. What the generator can say is the entire space of what any player can be called.
  • Clips carry no personal data by default. Generated clips show platoon colours and mole names, not player display names or account handles, and the share link resolves to a replay rather than a profile (§15).
  • Minimal account, neutral age gate. A neutral date-of-birth gate at first run; under-13 accounts (and equivalent thresholds elsewhere) get no email collection, no analytics beyond crash reporting, and no store access without a platform-level parental approval. No discoverable social graph, so nobody can be found by strangers.
  • Two lanes, split by age. Because the game is multiplayer only (§12), how a child gets into a match is a design decision rather than a settings screen. Local couch play is open to everyone and needs no account at all. Game codes are open to every age, because a match assembled from a code is a match with people the child already knows, and the person who gave them the code is accountable for who is in it. Random matchmaking with strangers is gated to accounts over the threshold, or to younger ones with platform-level parental approval. Nothing is taken away by that gate except strangers.
  • Strangers are unusually safe here. A matchmade lobby has no chat, no voice, no typed names, no profiles and no way to contact anybody afterwards. The entire attack surface that makes stranger matchmaking risky for children in other games simply does not exist, which is what makes offering it at all defensible.
  • Nothing that looks like gambling. No loot boxes, no random cosmetics, no timed pressure offers, no currency conversion layers: cosmetics are bought directly at a visible price, and a large slice is earnable by play (§19). This is also what keeps the store compliant in the jurisdictions that now treat loot boxes as gambling.
  • Ratings certification is scheduled, not hoped for. An IARC questionnaire dry run happens at M1 while content is still cheap to change, and again before Early Access. If PEGI insists on 7, that is accepted and the store copy says so plainly; what is not acceptable is discovering it at submission.
The trade being made

Cutting text chat costs something real: no trash talk, no coordinating a temporary alliance in a four-way match, less stickiness in Anytime pace. That is accepted deliberately. The emote wheel is designed to carry the social load instead (including a pointed "nice shot" and a very sarcastic "after you"), and couch play, where the chat is the room, is the flagship mode precisely because it needs none of this.

19Business model

The game is a free download on Steam and Google Play and monetises through cosmetics only: mole outfits and weapon reskins to start. No gameplay power, no ads, no loot boxes, no energy timers. A skill game about reading your opponents dies the moment anyone can buy an edge, so the wall between cosmetics and mechanics is absolute, and the no-classes rule (§4) means there is not even a hypothetical stat to sell.

  • Outfits: per-mole hats, uniforms, glasses, victory dances. Whole-platoon themed sets (Highland Regiment, Space Program, Office Workers) are the hero SKUs.
  • Weapon reskins: the Beetle Launcher as a party popper, the Big Whack as an inflatable hammer, confetti Clod Lobber. Reskins never change timing, arc, radius or any sound cue that carries gameplay information; a reskinned weapon must telegraph identically.
  • Knockout flourishes: cosmetic variants on the exits from §5, a golden stretcher, a confetti poof, a tiny brass band that plays you off. The most-watched 1.2 seconds in any clip, and therefore the most desirable cosmetic slot in the game.
  • Tunnel liners and fanfares: vanity patterns for your dug tunnels and custom victory stings. Cheap to make, and in a four-player match they are on screen in front of three opponents all game.
  • Earnable lane: a visible slice of the catalogue comes only from play. With no solo content to grind (§12), everything earnable comes out of real matches: Mole of the Match awards, milestones, and rotating challenges that ask for something specific to happen in a live game. Slower to earn than a puzzle ladder would be, and worth more when someone recognises it.
  • Direct purchase only. Everything is bought at a visible price with no randomness and no intermediate currency, which is a rating and compliance decision as much as an ethical one (§18).
  • Why free fits this design: four-player matches mean every player recruits three friends rather than one, a game code carries no purchase wall, and the clip pipeline (§15) turns the game's funniest moments into install links with the paid cosmetics visible in every frame. The risk is the liveops treadmill; the mitigation is scope honesty in §20 and a cosmetics pipeline built for small, regular drops rather than events.
