# Molehill Madness

A turn-based artillery game where everybody takes their turn at the same time.

Four players, four moles each, one shared eight-second clock. Every round all four plot a move at once, then every plan resolves together: shots have to lead moving targets, four ambushes collide mid-air, and the best-laid plan marches proudly into a crater that did not exist when it was drawn. At round eight a line of lava appears along the bottom of the map and starts climbing, then closes in from the sides, until there is nowhere left to be careful.

Steam and Android, cross-play, free, cosmetics only. (iOS is out of scope until after the MVP.) Multiplayer only: no campaign, no AI. No words anywhere in the game.

## Layout

```
sim/     MoleSim, the deterministic game, and its tests
tools/   headless CLI, terrain dumps, corpus runners, the art importer
client/  Godot 4 project (added at Phase 2)
relay/   ASP.NET Core lobby and turn-exchange service (added at Phase 4)
online/  the client's half of playing apart: lobbies, paces, emotes, the age band
clip/    the shareable round: the drama scorer and the encoders
art/     the generated sheets, as they arrived. The importer's input
docs/    design document, implementation plan
```

The architectural rule, from which everything else follows: **MoleSim is the game and the engine is a lens pointed at it.** MoleSim is a plain `netstandard2.1` library with no engine types, no I/O and no floating-point arithmetic anywhere. The client renders it, the relay ferries its plans around, the tools replay it. Nothing else may mutate its state.

## Determinism

Every device must compute the same match from the same inputs, or online play is impossible. Three rules make that testable rather than hopeful:

1. **Fixed-point maths only.** `Fix64` (Q48.16) throughout the simulation. IEEE 754 arithmetic varies between platforms and would silently fork a live match between a phone and a PC.
2. **One seeded generator per match.** `MatchRng` (xoshiro256\*\*), drawn in a defined order, so replays are bit-exact down to which knockout animation plays.
3. **A golden corpus that only grows.** Every fixed bug leaves a recorded match behind, replayed on every platform in CI. The corpus is the reviewer a solo project does not otherwise have.

## Building

Needs the .NET 10 SDK (the library targets `netstandard2.1`; tests and tools target `net10.0`).

```bash
dotnet build
dotnet test
```

## Status

**Phase 0 gate: passed.** The simulation computes identical results on Windows, Linux and Android, across two CPU architectures and two .NET runtime versions, and the probe runs in 22 ms at 60 fps on a phone. Full evidence and the deferred risks are in [`docs/phase-0-gate.md`](docs/phase-0-gate.md).

**Phase 1 complete.** MoleSim plays a whole match headlessly: seamless movement priced in stamina, the full fifteen-weapon arsenal, projectiles and blasts with line of sight, crates, lava, pacing and the knockout reel. `dotnet run --project tools/Molehill.Cli -- match` plays one to a winner and prints it round by round. A golden corpus of pinned match hashes is verified on every platform in CI.

**Phase 2 in progress.** The game is playable. `client/scenes/Match.tscn` plays a whole match: steer your mole and watch the gauges drain as it walks, aim and stamp the turn's shot, plant a charge if you fancy it, then watch every plan resolve at once over eight seconds. Craters appear when the shells land, damage numbers rise and fade where they hit, and moles leave on whichever of the eight exits they earned. 596 tests, across the simulation, the relay, the online client and the clip pipeline.

Turns are steered rather than drawn. The first version had the player draw the route as a line and looped a translucent ghost along it while the mole stood still, and the first time somebody who had not built it picked it up, they could not tell which mole was theirs or what the line was for. Steering says the same thing with one mole in it: push, and the mole walks, digging or climbing or falling as the ground demands, because the preview runs the real movement solver against a copy of the terrain. The waypoints it leaves behind are positions the mole genuinely stood at, which is a stronger guarantee than a hand-drawn line ever gave: `MoleMotion` has a stall-detector whose comment hoped that real routes would always be reachable, and steering makes that true by construction. Standing still costs nothing, so the eight seconds are eight seconds of walking rather than of wall clock, and a player who spends half a minute thinking has spent none of the round. The path is kept, for the markers and for a reset, and never drawn.

Planning is simultaneous, which is what split screen is for. Every platoon with its own controller plans at the same moment on one shared clock; platoons without one share the pointer and take turns, and the clock resets as it changes hands. The same code covers a couch full of gamepads and a prototype on one mouse, so the testable configuration is not a separate build. The screen carves into a pane per platoon while they plan.

A pluck bar is drawn over a mole's head only if it belongs to the platoon whose view it is. Sixteen bars on one screen is fifteen you cannot act on and one you can, which is the same argument as only showing a player their own plan. What everybody still sees is the tally, counting who is left standing, and the damage numbers, which say what a shot did the moment it lands. A replay camera belongs to a piece of the action rather than to a platoon, so it owns nothing and draws no bars at all.

The replay is cut by a director rather than by the seating plan. A round has already resolved before its first frame is drawn, so the whole shape of it is knowable up front: who was in it, where they went, and how far apart the separate pieces of it were. `ReplayDirector` groups the moles by proximity, works out how many cameras that needs, and decides how far back each stands, all once before the round plays, which is the only way to stop the screen splitting and merging every time somebody is punted sideways. The design's rule is that the camera "merges by proximity and splits when the fight spreads", and grouping by proximity is the half that was missing: the first version measured whether everything fitted one screen and otherwise fell back to a pane per platoon at a fixed zoom, so three moles in a scrum and one off on its own gave four panes, three of them showing the same hillside. Cameras frame what they are pointed at and track it, and the zoom never changes mid-round.

Two things fell out of framing the action rather than following one mole. A mole punted forty metres up used to take the camera with it until the ground left the frame and the pane went blank cream, which is correct behaviour and indistinguishable from a fault; a camera framed for the whole flight keeps the ground in shot. And a shot is framed to show eighteen metres of height or thirty-two of width, whichever the pane's proportions can manage, rather than both: demanding both made a wide stacked band show sixty-four metres across in order to satisfy its height, which is wider than the whole map and left two thirds of the frame empty.

The round has a moment, and the replay slows down and pushes in on it. The last knockout if there was one, because a knockout is what a round is about and they arrive at the end of one, which is the final impact the design asks for; failing that the heaviest hit, so a round nobody went out of still ends on something; failing both, nothing at all, since a round where four moles walked about and missed does not want a slow motion replay of the missing. The clock is scaled rather than the tick, so the moles, the terrain, the damage numbers, the cameras and the noises all slow together and stay in step, and playback still ends at the same point in the recording because it counts simulation seconds rather than real ones. Only the camera watching the mole it happens to pushes in. The others carry on at their own framing, which is what a gallery does: everybody rolls, one camera gets the shot. It is the one deliberate exception to a camera never re-framing mid-round, and it is the reason to have a director at all.

Each camera wears its broadcast furniture: viewfinder crop marks in the corners, a tally light that pulses while the round is playing, which camera it is as pips, and a caption of platoon colours saying whose fight is in shot. It is sports television for a reason beyond the look of it. A replay that cuts to two cameras has to say so or the second pane reads as a rendering fault, and a player watching four moles they cannot control needs to find their own in a hurry. All of it wordless, and the camera number is pips rather than a numeral, because the one numeral the design keeps is spent on damage.

The HUD is wordless. Every weapon and gauge is drawn from primitives in `Glyphs.cs` rather than imported, which scales to any screen without a sprite sheet per density, recolours per platoon for free, and leaves the prototype with no art dependency at all. Digits survive only for damage, which is the exception the design carves out on the grounds that a numeral reads the same in every language a seven-year-old might have. The planning clock is a ring for the same reason.

On a phone it collapses to one view with a stick under the left thumb and the controls the design specifies under the right: a weapon wheel you flick, a button to fire that doubles as the aim stick, one to plant, one to reset and one to commit. Since the stick took over movement, a drag on the map means what a drag on a map means everywhere else, so the camera pans with one finger and pinches with two, and the one gesture the game had invented for itself is gone. Touch reaches the same handful of verbs the keys do, so the rules cannot tell a thumb from a keyboard.

Nothing in the arsenal is free except the Clod Lobber. Every other weapon has a finite stock, comes back only from crates, and a plan naming something a platoon has none of is refused rather than degraded, which is the same anti-cheat rule as everything else: a client can submit rubbish, never a state nobody else has. That closes the other half of the crate loop, which until now threw weapon crates away for want of anywhere to put them. A hop is booked with one press for the moment it is pressed, which steering makes possible and drawing did not: a route has no "now" to book anything against.

The map is a third sky over two thirds of caved-out ground, and the caves go all the way down. They used to stop a fifth of the map short of the bedrock, which left the deepest sixth as undisturbed strata nobody had a reason to visit: the same objection that moved the surface down in the first place, made again one layer lower. The noise block that sets their scale is three times what it was as well, because at one mole across a chamber was somewhere to squeeze through rather than somewhere to go, and at three it is a passage two moles can pass in. The surface used to sit a quarter of the way down with undisturbed strata below it, which meant every route was along the top and most of the map was basement nobody reached. The underground is hollowed into caves by interpolated noise cut at a level, so there are chambers and passages of no particular size, which is what a cave looks like and what makes reaching one worth paying for. The caves stay buried under a roof of solid ground, because a spawn point is placed on the first solid cell in its column and a cave that broke the surface would quietly move where everybody starts. `MapMakerTests` defends that, along with the proportion of ground that ends up hollow: too little and the caves are scenery, too much and the stamina economy loses its teeth, since digging is the price the whole movement design is built around and ground somebody else already dug is free.

Getting caves that look like caves took three goes, and each failure looked entirely reasonable in the code. Noise at one weight per cell gives ground like pumice, because a smoothing pass can remove speckle but cannot grow a chamber, so every hole stays a cell or two across and a mole twelve cells wide cannot get into any of them. One bit per block fixes the scale and breaks the shape, because the blocks survive smoothing intact and the result is a right-angled maze on an eight-cell grid. Interpolating between the weights rather than stepping between them keeps the scale and loses the grid. There was a real bug in the middle of that too: the smoothing rule counted five solid cells out of the eight neighbours, which sounds like a majority and is not one, so ground that was half hollow eroded a little more on every pass and five passes left rubble floating in air. Counting the cell's own square makes five of nine an actual majority, which is stable.

Half the platoons start underground. Everybody used to spawn on the surface, which made the caves somewhere to go rather than somewhere anybody was, and meant the first minute of every match was fought along one line. The column each spawn stands in has not changed, only the height, and only from ledges that column actually has: a run of clear cells with something solid under it, which is the same thing whether the clear part is the sky or a chamber, so the surface comes out of the same scan as ledge zero without being a special case. A floor needs three solid cells under it before it counts as ground rather than as a lid over the next hole down, and fourteen clear above it, because a mole is twelve cells across. Chosen off the terrain's own hash rather than from a generator, which keeps it out of the map's random sequence entirely.

There are things standing about in the garden: pots, mushrooms, fallen logs, picket fences, gnomes and bird baths, scattered along the surface wherever the ground is flat enough to stand something on and clear of anywhere a mole might start. They are built out of terrain rather than drawn over it, which is why there is no rendering code for them at all. The shader fills ground and outlines wherever it meets air, so a pot made of cells arrives as an outlined pot for nothing, it is destructible on the same terms as everything else, and it cannot end up floating over a crater somebody made underneath it. The design's first biome is a garden with lawns, flowerbeds and a sprinkler and its second is an allotment where root vegetables are "chunky soft cover", so cover you can hide behind and blow apart is the point rather than decoration.

Three bugs in that, none of which showed up as an error. Nothing was placed at all to begin with, because the flatness test allowed four cells of surface variation across a footprint the waves move ten to twenty cells over, so every candidate spot was rejected; a test that counts what is standing on the surface now catches that directly. Then everything was placed in the outer margins of the map, because keeping fifteen cells clear of sixteen possible spawn columns across three player counts blocked nearly the whole span between them. And a fallen log's rounded ends were centred on its ends rather than tucked inside its width, so it reached eight cells further than anything had accounted for and came to be lying across a starting position that was supposed to be clear.

There is a second garden standing in the first, and this one is drawn rather than built. Tufts of grass, spoil heaps, flowers, a dandelion, a stone and a worm, scattered along the frozen skyline wherever it is flat enough to look planted and far enough from the last one to read as scattered. Pictures rather than cells, which is the opposite of the choice the pots and gnomes get and right for opposite reasons: those are cover, and building them out of terrain is what makes a mole able to hide behind one and a shell able to break it, whereas a tuft of grass that stopped a shell would be a surprise nobody could have planned around. Each one checks every frame that the cell it was planted in is still solid, so a crater takes its flowers with it instead of leaving them hanging over it, which is the fallen log's mistake and cheap enough to be worth not repeating. They live on their own node between the ground and the moles, because the scene point-samples everything so that the cell field reads as cells, and a tuft shrunk from a hundred and sixty pixels to sixty that way loses half its blades.

The last one is the most interesting: clutter fills air and leaves ground alone. A thing is planted on the lowest point of its own footprint, so on any slope its lower rows sit at or under the turf on the high side, and overwriting painted the turf away. That left columns with no turf in them at all, and the turf line is how everything else works out where the original ground was, including the test that guarantees the caves keep a roof over themselves.

The edges are smooth, and the grid is gone from the picture without going anywhere near the simulation. `terrain.gdshader` samples the cell grid as a field, blurs it across a cell and a half, and cuts it at a level, which gives a boundary that follows the cells without being made of them. That is the cartoon look this game wants and it is close to free: no contour extraction, no mesh to rebuild when somebody digs, and no per-frame work on the processor at all, since the dirty-region upload the client already did is the whole cost. It measures at 0.78 ms of processor and 0.36 ms of graphics a frame. The terrain stays a grid of cells everywhere it matters, because the simulation owns it, it is hashed, and every rule about digging and blasting is expressed in cells.

One pass covers the pane, backdrop included, which saves keeping a separate sky draw ordered behind the ground and makes anything off the edge of the map carry on as backdrop for free. It lives on a child node at a negative Z index rather than in the view's own drawing, because a canvas shader applies to everything a node draws and the view draws sixteen moles and a HUD as well.

The ground is soil now, and what is behind it is either countryside or more soil. Three textures do that: the destructible ground, the darker dirt behind a tunnel, and a strip of pale hills and hedgerows for the sky. It was one flat fill with an outline before, which was itself an improvement on the shade per material it started as, because five kinds of stripe on a map that is two thirds underground tells a player nothing they can act on and what matters about a cell is which side of an edge it is on. A flat fill says that much and nothing more, and in particular it cannot say whether the air on the far side of the edge is thirty metres of sky or the inside of a tunnel. The materials still decide what digging costs; they are still not what the picture is about. The dirty-region patch repaints one cell beyond the reported rectangle, because a cell turning to air changes how its neighbours draw, and without that every crater came out with no outline down its sides.

Which of the two a patch of air is comes from a split settled before round one and never touched again: one row per column, handed to the shader as a texture a texel wide per column. Both halves of that were tempting to skip and both matter. Read off the live terrain instead, a column's first solid cell drops thirty metres the moment somebody sinks a shaft from the lawn, so the backdrop behind the shaft turns from soil to sky and the tunnel reads as a canyon; blow the top off a hill and the sky follows the crater down. A backdrop that moves when the foreground is destroyed is not a backdrop. And one horizontal split for the whole map would cut across the hills, putting a band of soil in the sky over every summit. Sixteen bits a column rather than eight, for the same reason and at the same scale: eight would quantise a four-hundred-and-eighty-row map to about two rows, and two rows the wrong way is a dark halo along every skyline.

The layer behind is darkened, which is not a nicety. Both dirt textures came off the same generator within a few percent of each other in tone, so undarkened a cave read as an outline drawn on solid ground rather than as a hole in it, and a tunnel somebody had spent half their stamina digging was invisible from two metres away. That is the same class of fault as the invisible tunnels and the garden clutter huddled in the margins: nothing failed, and the frame looked almost right. Darkening what is behind is the whole reason depth reads in a flat picture, since an outline only says where a boundary is and not which side of it is air.

The rest of the sheets come through the same importer, and most of what it does is about the difference between a sheet and a sprite. Every sheet is a uniform grid, which was read off a gutter analysis of each one rather than assumed, and several carry the artist's own labels under each cell, so a cell can be cropped before it is trimmed. Thin things are dropped by a morphological opening on the alpha rather than by looking for shapes: it removes a cell border, a ground line under a mole's feet and a letter of a label, and keeps anything as thick as a claw, without needing any idea what any of it is. The radius is per sheet because it is a judgement about the thinnest thing worth keeping, and both ends of that were found the hard way. At three it left the ground lines, which turn out to be exactly seven pixels; at three it also removed the crate's landing marker outright, which is a dashed ellipse thinner than a claw.

An animation comes out as one strip with every frame the same size, and that is worth more than the file count. Trimmed frame by frame, each frame's own bounding box becomes its origin and the mole jitters as the animation plays. Cut instead with one rectangle for the whole set, the union of what every frame occupies, and the motion the artist drew is the only motion there is. A mole comes out four times, once per platoon, because four platoons are told apart entirely by colour and the trunks are the only part of the artwork that can carry one without repainting the animal. The pixel's own lightness is measured against the garment's average and reapplied to the target colour, so a dark fold stays a dark fold.

Which pixels are the trunks had to be measured rather than guessed, and the first attempt was visibly wrong. Inherited from the mole sheet script, the threshold was any strongly saturated red at 0.45 saturation and above, and this art's brown body sits at about a tenth of a hue turn with saturation up to 0.65, so the generator's own noise pushed thousands of body pixels over the line and every mole came out with green blotches all over it. Measured on the baseline sheet, the trunks are thirty-three thousand pixels at 0.75 and above averaging 187, 41, 39, and the body tops out at 0.65. Seventy separates them with room either side.

The fifteen weapon glyphs and the eight knockout exits needed no mapping work at all, which was a pleasant surprise: the artist drew the weapons in WeaponId order across three sheets, and the nine death sheets are the eight exits `KnockoutReel` has been choosing between since Phase 1 plus a plain launch that matches none of them. Three of the exits have their scenery drawn into the frames, a dirt wall and two ground bands, which is faithful to the sheets and will need thinking about when they are wired up.

The pixel work is a compiled helper inside the script rather than PowerShell. Loops were fine for the five ground sheets and are not fine for this: twenty-four sheets, a hundred and forty cells, an opening pass over each of them and four recolours of every mole is a couple of hundred million pixel operations, and at the speed the loops ran it measured at over twenty minutes against ten seconds now. `Add-Type` keeps that in one file with no new tool to install, and the check that the rewrite changed nothing it should not is that all nineteen textures already committed come out of it byte for byte identical.

The art's own proportions do not match this game's scale and cannot be made to. A mole is three quarters of a metre across on a map sixty metres wide, and the dirt was drawn with about five strata bands and a scatter of stones to a tile. Sized so a stone looks like a stone, the strata come out finer than turf; sized so the strata look like strata, every stone is a boulder twice the size of a mole. Six metres to a tile is the compromise, and it was arrived at by rendering frames and looking at them rather than by arithmetic, which is the only way that question has ever been answerable here.

The art is imported rather than used as it arrives, by `tools/scripts/import-art.ps1`, and the outputs are committed, because a build should not depend on a machine having an imaging library. Two of the three terrain images carry the generator's watermark, a soft four-pointed star in one corner, and on a texture that tiles across a sixty metre map that sparkle would appear in a grid and read as a rendering fault. The patch over it is a copy of the same rows from further along the texture, feathered in, with the offset chosen by trying a spread of them and keeping whichever both joins up at the border and brings the least with it. Both halves of that scoring earned their place: this dirt is banded, so a patch taken from the wrong offset puts a stratum line at the wrong height and the seam is worse than the star was, and a patch that happens to contain a light pebble swaps one bright spot for another, which left the check afterwards unable to tell whether it had worked.

Traps, snares and capped vents are visible, as suspicious little mounds of fresh soil in their owner's colour, outlined while they are still arming and gaining a solid eye once they are live. They were invisible until now, which was not a cosmetic gap but a broken rule: the design gives a trap a round's delay before it arms precisely so that it "sits there as a suspicious mound for a whole round first and opponents get to decide whether to respect it or test it", and against something nobody can see the delay buys nothing and the trap is a random punishment for walking somewhere. One shape for all three, because what matters is that something is there and whose it is. A placement now records the round and tick it went down, so a replay shows it appearing when its mole planted it rather than having it sit on the map from the first frame.

Which mole takes a platoon's turn is not a choice. The simulation already rotated through a platoon and skipped whoever had gone out, and there was briefly a key to step past that rotation, which is a decision nobody needs to be asked to make and one more thing to explain.

The aim is an arrow of fixed length that fills up as the throw charges, and the head fills in solid only at maximum. Both halves of it used to scale the arrow's length by the power instead, which is the same mistake the wind gauge made and was fixed for: a gauge scaled by its own value is unreadable exactly where reading it matters, so a fifth-power shot drew a stub half a mole long and nobody could tell a soft throw from a mis-click. The charge is drawn from the same function that builds the shot, clamps included, so the gauge cannot promise a throw the plan will not contain.

How hard it throws is how long the button is held. That used to be the length of the drag, so pointing further away threw harder, which spent one gesture on two decisions: direction and power could not be adjusted without disturbing each other, and on a pad or a thumb, where a stick has a fixed throw, the whole useful range of powers lived in the last few millimetres of travel. Time is the honest axis for a wind-up and it is what the genre already uses. Point where you want it to go, hold to decide how hard, let go to stamp the shot. All three inputs say it the same way now, the mouse with its right button, the pad with its stick held out and the thumb with the fire button, and each draws the same filling ring the reset already had, because a hold on this screen has to announce itself somehow and there is no reason for it to speak twice.

It deliberately does not predict where the shell lands. The design describes a threat preview drawing the arc and blast radius, and the arc could be drawn honestly, since the projectile solver is deterministic and would run against a terrain copy the way the movement preview does. An artillery game whose shots are pre-plotted is a different game, though: direction and charge are what the player chose, and where it ends up is the round's answer.

Bracing is gone, having briefly been a button. It began as an action that only stopped a mole walking, which is exactly what planning nothing already does, so it was a choice with no consequence; it was then given a third off the next blast to make it worth pressing, which turned it into a reward for staying put in a game whose stalemate nudge exists to fight bunkering. Removing it settles that: planning nothing still braces in place, as the design says it should, and there is simply nothing to press for it. Worth recording that the design names five verbs, move, hop, aim, fire and brace, so the design document and the build now disagree by one until the document is updated.

A dozen findings so far, and not one of them came from a test. Ten came from watching rounds render, the most recent three being a menu whose spoil heap floated in the sky with the moles balanced on it because the heap and the hillside were drawn at two unrelated heights, choices that were a fifteen percent wash of ink over a tan hillside and so not visibly controls at all, and a clock, wind gauge and running tally left showing through the final scoreboard, which is three dead instruments behind a result. Craters used to be as wide as the blast that made them, which left the map unrecognisable by round five and contradicted pacing the design had already fixed, so a crater is now its own number and `ArsenalTests` defends the map surviving a dozen rounds. A round resolves before its first frame is drawn, so anything read from live state during playback gives the ending away: the map and the score are both replayed from the recording instead. And a gauge scaled by its own value is unreadable at low values, which is why the wind arrow keeps its length and puts the strength in the streaks behind it.

Three more came from the camera work: the blank pane and the empty band described above, and the shared tally, which is centred horizontally and so sits exactly where a two-by-two split puts its vertical seam, on top of the right-hand pane's own instruments. Its clearance is derived from the same two numbers that strip is built from, so the two cannot drift apart the next time either is retuned.

The other two came from somebody holding the phone. The ghost was the whole planning model and it was the single most confusing thing on the screen, which no test could have told me and no rendered frame did either, because a frame of it looks exactly as intended. And bracing turned out to be booked at the moment the button went down, which was a quiet trap: braced early, it cancelled the mole's own input partway along and silently truncated the walk its owner was still steering. That one is moot now, the action having been removed outright, but it is the reason the removal was easy to argue for.

There is a menu, and a way back to it. The match used to be the whole application: it started when the process did, and when it ended the only way to play again was to close the thing and open it again, which is a poor answer to a phase gate worded "four humans laughing and asking for a rematch". The menu is the design's couch lobby and nothing more: two, three or four platoons, chosen up front with four the default, shown as that many moles rather than as a numeral. A finished match puts up a scoreboard instead of a single glyph in the middle of the screen, ordered by the only ranking a free-for-all has, which is the winner and then whoever lasted longest, with a flower for whoever took the flowerbed and hollow rings for the moles who were carried off. One press anywhere goes back to the menu, held off for a moment first so the press that skipped the last replay cannot dismiss a result nobody has read.

Damage taken rather than dealt, because that is what the simulation records: a hit names its victim and nothing anywhere names who threw the thing. Recording the thrower would mean carrying attribution through every blast, gusher and lava bounce for the sake of a scoreboard.

All eight knockout exits are drawn. The simulation has chosen between them since Phase 1 and recorded which, and the client drew two: the stretcher squad, and spin-and-poof for everything else, so six deterministic and already-recorded outcomes were rendering as the wrong joke. Nobody dies in this game, they are carried off, sat down among cartoon birds, inflated and zipped off on a raspberry, punched through a wall leaving a mole-shaped hole, launched out of frame leaving a helmet spinning, dropped through the floor delighted, or taken by a burst of steam. The reel picks by cause rather than at random, which the design is explicit about: the floor giving way underneath is a different joke from sitting down among birds, and which one happened is knowable. It matters more than most presentation work because the gate is a question about whether the game is funny and this is the game's main answer to it.

Sound is synthesised at startup with no audio assets at all: seven waveforms built from sine, square and noise, played off the same recording the damage numbers come from so each lands on the tick it belongs to. It exists because the gate asks whether the slapstick is funny, and silent slapstick is not the same thing being tested.

`--demo` drives the game through its own interface without a player, walking through the menu and starting the next match from the scoreboard so a long run soaks the whole loop rather than one match. `--frail` starts everybody nearly out so the knockouts can be watched, `--seed=N` replays one particular garden, `--split` forces the cameras apart, `--touch` brings up the phone controls on a desktop, and `--mute` shuts it up. With Godot's `--write-movie` they make the render layer inspectable frame by frame, which is how ten of the twelve findings turned up:

```bash
godot --path client --position 9000,9000 --write-movie frames/f.png --fixed-fps 30 --quit-after 300 -- --demo --split
```

The window position is not decoration. Movie recording needs a rendering context, so `--headless` crashes, and a verification loop that opens a window every run buries whatever you were working in. Off the side of the desktop it records exactly the same frames and stays out of the way.

For a phone, `tools/scripts/deploy-android.sh` exports a signed debug APK, checks that the C# actually made it in, and installs it if a device is connected. Pass `--export-only`, or just leave the phone unplugged, and it hands over the APK to sideload instead. USB debugging is not required.

A clip comes out as a video where there is anything to encode one with, and as an animated PNG where there is not. The encoder is a seam rather than a function now, begin and add and finish, and that shape is the point rather than tidiness: the pipeline used to gather every frame into a list and hand the list over, which is what an APNG needs and what makes a hardware encoder impossible, since an encoder that consumes frames as they arrive can never be written against a signature that wants them all at once. A full portrait frame is eight megabytes, so the difference is two frames in memory against three hundred and seventy megabytes of them, and it is the reason the fallback has to be handed shrunken frames while the video path takes them at full size. Which one is in use is decided before the first frame is rendered, by encoding two sixteen-pixel frames and seeing whether they come out, because a streaming encoder does not keep what it has been given and one that failed halfway would have nothing left to fall back to but the whole re-simulation again.

The desktop encoder is ffmpeg, found on the path or beside the game, fed raw RGBA down a pipe. Its command line is a static that returns a string, so a test can read it back without launching anything, which matters because nearly every way this can be wrong is in that string and most of them fail quietly: a pixel format that does not match the frames produces coloured noise, a size that does not match produces a picture that shears sideways, and anything but 4:2:0 produces a video that encodes perfectly and then will not play on a phone. Where a real ffmpeg exists the encoder is run for real and the bytes are checked, since ffmpeg is the only thing that actually knows whether the line is right, and CI installs one on the Linux runner so that every push does it. That was not free: the tests skip themselves where there is no binary, GitHub's image does not carry one, and a suite that looked green had quietly never run the encoder. Android's MediaCodec is not here: it needs a Godot plugin written in Java, which needs a device to mean anything, and iOS is out of scope entirely.

The menu has a fourth table: strangers. It is the only one of the four that needs an account, because it is the only one that puts a player with somebody they did not invite, and it is dimmed rather than hidden for an account under the threshold, the same way a spent dynamite button is. An option that vanishes reads as a layout that moved; one that is visibly there and visibly unavailable reads as a rule, which is what it is. The design is explicit that "nothing is taken away by that gate except strangers", so the couch and the game code are untouched.

Waiting in the pool is its own screen rather than the lobby with the code missing, and drawing it found three things no test would have. The strangers glyph is drawn in the panel ink with its moles cut out in the paper colour, which is right on a panel and, on the open sky, is a white ghost with two red specks where the noses are; the row of seats hung off a fraction of the screen landed exactly on the skyline at 16:9, where a pale ring on the grass line is half a ring; and the offer of the other pace sat where the sweeping pulse lives, so the bar ran through the middle of the button and out the other side, which reads as a progress bar filling it. All three were found by looking at frames, and the last one only because a run went on long enough for the offer to appear at all.

Four panes at sixty frames a second is measured rather than assumed now, and it passes with about five times the headroom it needs: three milliseconds of a sixteen millisecond budget at 1080p, on a laptop with an entry level discrete card. `--perf` drives a match, turns vertical sync off, and prints frame times by beat and pane count, because a rate read off a window is the rate the display allowed and says nothing about what was left over. [`docs/perf.md`](docs/perf.md) has the numbers, the method, and the noise floor, which turns out to be the useful part: three runs of one configuration spread from 456 to 617 frames a second, so a difference smaller than a quarter is not a difference and every comparison wants repeats.

Two of the answers were about measuring rather than about the game. A run has to be on a screen, because a window that is minimised or moved off the desktop stops being drawn while still producing a full set of plausible timings, so the probe refuses a run with no draw calls in it; and the window goes on the laptop panel by way of Godot's own screen list rather than off the side of the world, since Windows and Godot count screen pixels differently and the old habit put development runs on top of whatever was on the monitors.

Nothing needed tuning as a result, which is its own answer: the frame rate is the same at 720p and at 1080p and the graphics time is a fifth of a millisecond either way, so this machine is bound by processor work per frame rather than by anything on the screen. The ground shader got two changes anyway, one of them a real bug: it was reading mipmapped textures inside branches, which is undefined in GLSL and left the level of detail up to the driver, and it was reading both sides of the ground boundary everywhere on screen when most of a frame is well inside one side or the other. A five tap version of the blur is there behind `--quality=low` and nothing selects it, because on this hardware it is not measurably cheaper and a phone has not been asked yet.

Still owed: the gamepad axis reads have never met real hardware, though the simultaneous planning and the plan verbs they feed are exercised by the driver. The pinch has not either, since a mouse has only ever had one finger. And the structured playtests, which are the actual gate. No amount of this code can answer whether it is funny.
